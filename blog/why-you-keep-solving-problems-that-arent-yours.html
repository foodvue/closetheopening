Why You Keep Solving Problems That Aren't Yours (And How to Stop)

7 min read

She asked if you had a minute.

You were in the middle of something, but she seemed stuck, so you said sure. She explained the situation. You gave her two quick thoughts. She thanked you. Then she asked a follow-up question. That question led to another. Somewhere around the third question, you realized you weren't advising anymore. You were doing the work.

She said that would be so helpful. She said she didn't want to mess it up. She said you were so much better at this than she was. And before you fully registered what had happened, you were restructuring her entire project on a Tuesday night while your own priorities sat untouched.

She wasn't being malicious. She probably didn't even realize she was doing it. But that doesn't change the fact that you walked into a five-minute conversation and walked out with someone else's workload.

If this sounds familiar, it's not because you're unlucky with the people in your life. It's because you have a pattern. And people have learned how to use it.

Why You Do This

Somewhere along the way, you learned that being useful was the path to being valued. Maybe you were the competent one in a chaotic family. Maybe your worth felt connected to your ability to handle things. Maybe you just noticed early on that people treated you differently when you could solve their problems, and you kept doing it because it worked.

The result is a specific kind of wiring. You see a problem, and your brain starts solving it before you've decided whether it's yours to solve. Someone describes a difficult situation, and you feel the pull to step in before you've asked yourself whether stepping in is appropriate. The gap between hearing about a problem and owning it is almost nonexistent.

This is what the Close The Opening framework calls The Fixer. It's one of seven Entry Points where psychological pressure consistently enters conversations.

The opening isn't that you're helpful. The opening is that you can be given endless problems to solve, and you'll take them. Not because you should. Because not taking them feels worse than taking them does.

That's the part most people miss. Seeing a problem and not solving it creates more discomfort for you than solving problems that aren't yours. So you keep solving. And the people around you keep bringing you more.

The Tactics That Target Fixers

Once you understand the pattern, the tactics that exploit it become predictable. Four of them show up repeatedly.

Helplessness Theater is the most common. Someone approaches you with a task they've been assigned. "I just can't figure this out," they say. "I've tried everything. You're so much better at this than I am." You look at the task. It's not that complicated. You could explain it in ten minutes. Or you could just do it in five. You're already mentally solving it before they've finished talking.

They don't have to ask you directly. They don't have to argue. They just perform incapability in your presence, and your instincts fill the vacuum.

Crisis Manufacturing is the one that bypasses your filters entirely. It's 9pm. Your phone buzzes. "I need to talk to you right now. It's urgent." You call. The crisis turns out to be a disagreement with a coworker. Or anxiety about a decision that doesn't need to be made until next week. It's not nothing. But it's not a 9pm emergency. Still, you're on the phone now, giving advice, problem-solving. An hour passes. This has happened before. It always seems urgent at first. It rarely turns out to be.

Urgency bypasses your normal judgment. When something is framed as a crisis, you don't pause to ask whether it's really your problem. You just respond.

Partial Effort Displays are harder to spot because they come wrapped in something that looks like initiative. A family member asks for help with their taxes. You suggest a few resources. A week later, they come back: "I tried that website you mentioned. I spent hours on it. I got through most of it, but I'm stuck on this last section. Can you just finish it?" They tried. They struggled. They almost got there. Refusing now feels like punishing their effort. So you take over. Except the remaining piece is the actual work. Everything before it was filling in boxes.

Responsibility Drift is the slowest and the hardest to confront. A coworker asks you to review their report. A quick favor. You do it. The next report comes to you automatically. No one asked. It was just sent. A month later, your manager says: "Can you make sure those reports are cleaned up before they go out?" When did this become your job? It happened through accumulated assumptions, none of which you explicitly agreed to. First you helped. Then you helped again. Then it was expected.

Why It's Hard to See While It's Happening

The reason Fixers stay stuck in this cycle isn't a lack of intelligence. It's that the pattern rewards you in the short term. You feel capable. You feel needed. You get the satisfaction of watching someone's problem dissolve because you stepped in. People thank you. People rely on you. People tell you you're incredible.

None of that is fake. The gratitude is real. The dependence is real too.

The cost shows up later. It shows up as resentment you can't quite explain. As a workload that keeps expanding while your job description stays the same. As a growing sense that you're a resource rather than a person. You do more, they do less, and eventually the imbalance becomes impossible to ignore, but also impossible to articulate without sounding like you're complaining about being helpful.

That's the trap. The pattern is invisible precisely because it looks like a good quality. Who would complain about being the person everyone trusts to solve things?

You would. At 11pm on a Tuesday. Working on something that was never yours to begin with.

How to Stop

Stopping doesn't mean becoming cold or unhelpful. It means recognizing the moment when helping stops serving the relationship and starts serving the pattern.

The key is in how you respond to the initial pull. That moment when someone describes a problem and your brain is already three steps into solving it. That's the opening. Everything that follows depends on what you do in that window.

For Helplessness Theater, the interrupt is simple: "I think you can figure this out." Express confidence in their ability. Do not offer to take over. This returns the task to its rightful owner and breaks the cycle that turns their discomfort into your responsibility.

For Crisis Manufacturing: "That sounds stressful. What's your plan?" Acknowledge the feeling without adopting the urgency. This returns problem-solving to them without dismissing what they're going through.

For Partial Effort Displays: "Sounds like you're making progress." Acknowledge the effort without joining it. This keeps the responsibility where it belongs and prevents their almost-finished work from becoming your job.

For Responsibility Drift: "I want to make sure I'm prioritizing correctly. Is this something you want me to treat as part of my role, or was this meant to stay occasional support?" Name the drift without accusation. This forces the transfer of responsibility into the open before a favor quietly becomes an expectation.

These will feel uncomfortable the first time you use them. That discomfort is the pattern pushing back. The resistance you feel when you read "I think you can figure this out" and it feels dismissive, like you're abandoning someone who needs help, that resistance is the belief that if you can help, you should. Regardless of what it costs you.

That belief is the opening. And the discomfort you feel when you challenge it is how you know you're closing it.

The Real Shift

This week, when someone presents you with a problem, try waiting five seconds before responding. Ask yourself one question: what would happen if I didn't help?

If the honest answer is "they would figure it out eventually," try saying: "That sounds difficult. What are you going to do?" Then stop.

You're not withholding. You're not being cruel. You're returning the problem to the person it belongs to. And you're giving them something more valuable than a solution: the chance to build the capability they'll never develop as long as you keep stepping in.

The pattern runs until you see it. And once you see it, the five-minute favor that always turns into an hour stops working the same way.
